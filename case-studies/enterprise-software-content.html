Context

Enterprise buyers usually need clarity before they need persuasion.

A question about implementation cost can involve licensing, consultants, internal staffing, training, configuration, project scope, and post-launch support. A question about digital adoption can span employee behaviour, software utilisation, training, process efficiency, and ROI.

The content had to keep the useful complexity without making the reader decode enterprise-software language just to get an answer.

What I owned

I had to translate both the software and the decision around it.

The research was not only about definitions. I needed to understand the factors that change the decision: who is involved, what creates cost, where implementation risk appears, why adoption fails, what the buyer needs to compare, and how the product fits into that problem.

What the page needed to translate

Technical detailPlatform, implementation, workflow, and software-usage concepts.
Operational impactTraining, productivity, process friction, and adoption risk.
Commercial impactCost, resources, ROI, support, and buying objections.
Readable answerA structure that lets the buyer understand the decision without decoding jargon.

What the buyer needed

The strongest topics were close to real enterprise decisions.

Understand

What is happening?

Digital adoption, software utilisation, workflow problems, employee friction, and category education.

Assess

What will this involve?

Implementation scope, training, consultants, customisation, support, change management, and risk.

Evaluate

Is the investment worth it?

Cost, ROI, productivity, utilisation, solution fit, and the business case for moving forward.

How I wrote it

Answer the buyer’s question first, then make the product connection.

The ServiceNow implementation-cost page, for example, could not stop at one price number. A useful answer needed to explain the variables that change the cost. Digital-adoption content had a similar challenge: explain why employees struggle with software, what low usage costs the business, and what better adoption changes.

  • Start with the specific question the buyer is trying to answer.
  • Break the answer into the variables that actually change the outcome.
  • Explain the operational and commercial implications in plain language.
  • Bring the product into the answer where it genuinely helps.

What I took from it

Complex B2B content is partly a writing problem and partly an information-design problem.

The reader needs a direct answer, the page needs enough context to be useful, and the business needs the topic to connect naturally to the product. The job is making those three things work together without turning the page into a wall of software language.
