Explaining complex enterprise software without making the reader work for it.

Context

Enterprise buyers usually need clarity before they need persuasion.

A question about implementation cost can involve licensing, consultants, internal staffing, training, configuration, project scope and post-launch support. A question about digital adoption can span employee behaviour, software utilisation, training, process efficiency and ROI.

That complexity creates a content problem: if the page is too shallow, it loses trust. If it is too dense, the reader has to work harder than necessary. The job was to keep the useful complexity while making the decision easier to understand.

My role

I had to translate both the software and the decision around it.

For enterprise content, research is not only about collecting definitions. It is about identifying the factors that change the decision: who is involved, what creates cost, where implementation risk appears, why adoption fails, what the buyer needs to compare, and how the product fits into that problem.

What the content had to translate

Technical detailPlatform, implementation, workflow and software-usage concepts
Operational impactTraining, productivity, process friction and adoption risk
Commercial impactCost, resources, ROI, support and buying objections
Reader answerA structure that lets the buyer understand the decision without decoding jargon

Assets created

The work covered both category education and high-intent buyer content.

The asset mix changed with the decision the reader was trying to make. Some pieces taught the category. Others answered questions much closer to implementation, budget, adoption, and ROI.

Implementation cost guidesLong-form assets covering licensing, consultants, staffing, training, customisation, support and other cost drivers.

Commercial-intent SEO pagesSearch-focused content designed around questions closer to implementation, cost and solution evaluation.

Buyer decision map

The strongest topics sat close to real enterprise questions.

Understand

What is happening?

Digital adoption, software utilisation, workflow problems, employee friction and category education.

Assess

What will this involve?

Implementation scope, training, consultants, customisation, support, change management and risk.

Evaluate

Is the investment worth it?

Cost, ROI, productivity, utilisation, solution fit and the business case for moving forward.

The page should meet the reader at the question they already have. Product relevance can then be introduced in the part of the answer where it is genuinely useful.

Content approach

Answer the buyer’s question first, then make the product connection.

The ServiceNow implementation-cost content, for example, needed to go beyond a single price number. The useful answer includes the variables that change the cost. Digital-adoption content similarly works better when it explains why employees struggle with software, what low usage costs the business, and what a better adoption process changes.

A simplified enterprise-content structure

QuestionStart with the specific thing the buyer needs to understand
VariablesBreak the answer into the factors that actually change the outcome
Trade-offsExplain cost, risk, resources, workflow or implementation implications
Next stepConnect the solution naturally after the decision context is clear

Checks I used in this kind of work

  • Does the page answer the actual cost, adoption, implementation or ROI question?
  • Are important variables explained instead of hidden behind generic statements?
  • Would a non-specialist business reader understand the logic without losing accuracy?
  • Does the product connection appear where it helps the reader, not simply where marketing wants a CTA?

What this shows

Complex B2B content is an information-design problem as much as a writing problem.

The reader needs a clear answer, search needs enough context to understand the page, and the business needs the topic to connect naturally to the product. Good enterprise content makes those three requirements work together rather than forcing the reader through a wall of software language.

That is why complex products are a useful fit for how I work: research deeply enough to understand the moving parts, then simplify the explanation without oversimplifying the decision.
